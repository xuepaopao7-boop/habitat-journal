Workspace

The small lighting shift that changes a workday.

Good office light is layered, adjustable and designed around the work—not centred on a single bright ceiling fixture.

By Habitat Journal editors·Published July 28, 2026·Updated August 1, 2026·8 min read
Research transparency: This independent workspace guide draws on public lighting and daylighting guidance. It is not a photometric plan for a specific room and currently contains no affiliate links.

A home office can have an excellent desk and supportive chair yet remain strangely tiring. Often the missing piece is light. One strong source creates glare and deep shadows; a dim room asks the eyes to keep adjusting between a bright screen and a dark background.

The solution is not simply more brightness. It is a useful relationship between daylight, focused task light and softer illumination for the room around you.

Place the desk across the window

When possible, sit perpendicular to the main window. This provides broad daylight without placing a glowing rectangle directly behind the screen or in your line of sight. Right-handed writers may prefer daylight from the left, with the reverse for left-handed writers, to reduce hand shadows.

Sheer curtains or an adjustable blind can soften sharp afternoon sun while preserving useful ambient light.

Watch the room over an entire workday before moving heavy furniture. East- and west-facing windows can change from gentle light to direct glare within a few hours. The most useful arrangement is the one that remains controllable when the sun is at its least convenient angle.

Give task light a specific job

A task lamp should illuminate the keyboard, notebook or material you are handling without reflecting in the screen. An adjustable arm and shielded shade matter more than decorative complexity.

The best task light is bright where the work happens and quiet everywhere else.

Place the lamp to the opposite side of your writing hand and slightly forward of your shoulder. If it creates a bright hotspot, bounce it from a pale wall or choose a wider, diffused shade.

A lamp with a stable base, useful reach and controls you can find without looking will often outlast a more elaborate model. Check whether the head stays in position, whether the switch is accessible from the chair and whether the lowest useful setting is genuinely low.

Light the background

A low lamp on a shelf, a shaded floor lamp or a soft wall wash reduces the contrast between the screen and the rest of the room. This can make evening work feel less severe and gives video calls a more natural background.

Keep the brightest source out of the camera frame. For calls, a broad light slightly above eye level is usually more forgiving than a small exposed lamp. If daylight comes strongly from one side, a dim lamp on the other can reduce the difference without flattening the room.

Coordinate colour, then keep it adjustable

Matching every lamp to the exact same colour temperature is less important than avoiding an obvious conflict. A warm shelf lamp beside a much bluer desk lamp can make the room feel disjointed. Selectable-colour fixtures are useful when they make it easy to tune the room once; constant colour changes are rarely the point.

Dimming is more valuable than maximum brightness. Daylight, screen content and the task on the desk all change, so the room should be able to respond. Separate control of task and ambient light gives that flexibility without turning the ceiling into the only answer.

Check the screen instead of the lamp

With the usual documents and applications open, look at the screen from your working position. Reflections, visible lamp shapes and bright windows are clues that the source needs to move or soften. A monitor hood is rarely the first fix; placement, blinds and indirect light usually deserve attention first.

Then look away from the screen toward the wall behind it. If that area feels almost black compared with the display, add a low, shielded source or wall wash. The aim is not a uniformly bright room, but a gentler transition across the field of view.

Change the room at the end of work

Lighting can also create a boundary. Use brighter, cooler task lighting during focused hours, then switch it off when work ends and leave only a warm ambient source. The physical change helps the same room return to domestic life.

A better workspace rarely requires a complete setup overhaul. Begin with the light you have, move it with purpose and add only the layer that is missing.

A one-evening lighting audit

  • Photograph the screen from the chair to reveal reflections and bright windows.
  • Move the desk or monitor perpendicular to the dominant daylight where practical.
  • Give the desk an adjustable, shielded task source.
  • Add one soft background source if the screen sits in a dark field.
  • Check the camera view with the lights you actually use for calls.
  • Make task and ambient layers separately controllable.

Quick test

Turn off the ceiling light. Add one source for the desk and another for the room behind it. Notice where glare disappears.
